# 30 High-Engagement LinkedIn Posts for B2B sales reps

> 30 ready-to-use LinkedIn posts for B2B sales reps, with practical prompts to spark engagement and start sales conversations.

B2B sales reps often know what they want to say on LinkedIn but struggle to make it useful to someone who does not already know them. The posts that earn attention tend to start with a real sales moment, offer a specific lesson, and leave room for conversation. This guide gives you a repeatable way to write them without turning every post into a pitch.

## A simple structure for a useful post

Try this sequence:

1. **Opening:** Name a situation your audience recognizes.
2. **Detail:** Add a concrete example, contrast, or small story.
3. **Takeaway:** Share one practical lesson.
4. **Invitation:** Ask a relevant question or offer a low-pressure next step.

Keep one post focused on one idea. If the draft contains three lessons, split it into three posts. Specificity matters more than grand claims: “A prospect asked us to send pricing before the discovery call” is more credible—and easier to picture—than “Sales is changing faster than ever.”

## Four templates to adapt

Replace the bracketed text with details from your own work. Remove anything you cannot support with a real example.

### 1. The objection lesson

> “We already have a provider.”
>
> A prospect said this on a call last week. Instead of launching into a comparison, I asked what their current provider was doing well.
>
> That answer changed the conversation. We learned [specific unmet need], and could decide whether it was worth exploring further.
>
> A useful sales question can beat a fast rebuttal. What question helps you understand an objection?

Use this when you can share how a conversation unfolded. Keep the customer anonymous unless you have permission to name them.

### 2. The small process improvement

> We changed one step in our [sales process]: [specific change].
>
> Before, [what the old step looked like]. Now, [what happens instead].
>
> The result so far: [measured outcome, or a clear observation].
>
> It is a small adjustment, not a universal playbook. If you are improving [related process], what have you tried?

Use a real metric only if you can explain how it was measured. If you do not have one, describe the observable change rather than inventing a number.

### 3. The “what I got wrong” post

> I used to think [common assumption].
>
> Then [specific experience] made me reconsider it.
>
> Now I [changed behavior], because [reason or result].
>
> I still think [reasonable nuance]. What has changed your mind about [topic]?

A thoughtful correction can be more useful than a polished success story. Keep the focus on what you learned, not on performing humility.

### 4. The practical checklist

> Before a first call with a [type of buyer], I check:
>
> - [Relevant business context]
> - [Likely priority or challenge]
> - [One informed question]
>
> This helps me spend less time reciting a pitch and more time finding out whether there is a fit.
>
> What would you add to the list?

A short checklist works best when each item helps the reader take an action. Avoid turning it into a disguised product feature list.

## Make the post sound like you

Draft the post as you would explain the point to a colleague. Then edit for clarity:

- Replace broad claims with a moment, example, or observation.
- Cut the opening sentence if it only announces that the post is important.
- Use short paragraphs so the post is easy to scan.
- Keep the invitation connected to the topic; a useful question is often enough.
- Remove confidential customer details and get consent before sharing identifiable stories.

Read the final draft aloud. If it sounds like a press release or a script, simplify it. A post can be professional without being stiff, and it can invite a sales conversation without asking every reader to book a demo.

## A quick review before publishing

Ask yourself:

- Can the intended reader tell this is relevant to them?
- Does the post offer a useful idea even if nobody buys anything?
- Are the details accurate and appropriate to share?
- Is there one clear point?
- Would I be comfortable discussing this claim in a follow-up comment?

If the answer to the first two questions is no, revise the example or takeaway. Consistent, relevant posts build recognition over time; no single format guarantees engagement.
